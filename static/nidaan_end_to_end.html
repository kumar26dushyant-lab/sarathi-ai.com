Finding 1 — the conversion gap

We have told 46 people we can fight their claim. Sixteen paid. Twenty-seven are sitting there, review delivered, L2 unpaid, going nowhere. Every one has already consumed an expert review and produced no revenue. Today nothing owns that moment.

Finding 2 — the attribution gap

The claims table already has intermediary_code, channel_partner_id and associate_referrer. All three are used by exactly zero claims. Only branch_code is populated — so advisor chains and CP commissions are not being captured by the system at all. Every day that continues is attribution data lost permanently.

Also worth knowing

nidaan_claims.stage exists and reads intake on all 64 rows — added and never driven. The real state is carried by status, which is why “where is this case” is hard to answer. This proposal finishes scaffolding that already exists.

What one closed case file shows

Read this as one example, not the pattern

Case 800049 is referenced throughout because it is the only complete file available — start to finish, with every date and every rupee. It is a useful reference, not a template: no two claims run the same way, and nothing here should be read as “this is how cases go”. It is used to show what the system must be able to hold, not to predict what cases do.

That case — Star Health, ₹2,04,435 claimed, won at ₹1,44,357 — ran eighteen months. Against a four-to-five month target, that is the gap this design has to close.

Silences nobody counted

Eight weeks with no entry at all. Ten weeks between the hearing and the complainant being paid. Seven more before Nidaan was paid.

Decisions from memory

“no any respond by the company so that this case moved for lokpal registration” — a person noticed. The escalation paper makes that a rule with three dated reminders.

Facts buried in free text

Hearing dates, annexure numbers, who owes the next reply, and a live commission dispute all sit inside remark text where nothing can act on them.

03

Attribution, and who earns what

A claim can be brought by up to three parties in sequence. Today the system records one. That is why CP commissions live outside it.

ChainExampleDoors
Complainant onlySomeone finds the site and subscribesA1, A2, C1, C2
Referrer → complainantA branch code brings a subscriberB1, B2, B4, S1, S2, S4
Referrer → advisor → complainantBranch gives its link to an advisor, who brings their own clientsB3, S3
Referrer → CP → complainantWalk-in where a channel partner is also owedB5, S5

Advisor and channel partner are different things

PartyWho they areWhat that means for us
AdvisorAn insurance advisor who takes a subscription — silver, gold or platinum. They are a paying customer of ours who happens to bring claimsNo review fee, no L2 fee. Their claims flow straight through. We earn the settlement fee at the end
Channel partnerSomeone who does not want a subscription — either not an advisor at all, or an advisor who wants to stay off a plan. They pass leads to a branch or staff member and prefer to stay in the backgroundNo plan revenue. They earn a share of the settlement, and their visibility is deliberately limited
Settled — the branch rule

A branch earns for referring the advisor — the referral and the advisor’s recurring subscription. It earns nothing on that advisor’s claims. Those belong to the advisor.

Subscribers take no share of award money. A subscription buys the service; it does not buy a stake in outcomes.

Commission is configured, not coded

Settled — and this changes how it gets built

Percentages must be editable by a super-admin, per party, from ops. A super-admin decides who is in the calculation and who is not; removing a party makes the system recalculate the rest rather than leaving a hole.

That means commission is a rules table plus a calculator, never percentages written into code. Every calculation shows its working — who was included, at what rate, on what base — so a disputed share can be explained rather than argued about. And every change to a rate is recorded with who made it and when: rates that move quietly are how commission disputes start.

The design point

Store an ordered chain on the claim, not a single referrer: [referrer, intermediary?] with a role on each — branch, staff, advisor, CP. Settlement walks the chain and applies each party’s own rule. The fields already exist and are unused; this is filling in scaffolding, not new schema.

Still open (Q1)

The default percentages themselves — what an advisor, a branch, a staff member and a channel partner each get out of the settlement fee — and whether a case can carry both an advisor and a channel partner. The engine does not care what the numbers are; it does need to know the starting set.

04

The model: stage, blocker, flags

One case record, three independent fields. The brief was clear: the system should drive, and people should just do their work. This is how that becomes possible.

Stage one value

Where the case sits in the legal journey. What a manager means by “where is that case”.

Blocker one value

What we are waiting for right now. When it is none, we owe the next move.

Flags any number

What is wrong or urgent: documents short, originals missing, reminder overdue, hearing this week, unanswered mail, commission disputed, filing window closing.

Why this is what “system-driven” means in practice

Nobody has to decide what to work on. Every case with blocker = none is somebody’s job today, sorted by how long it has waited. Every flag is a specific thing that went wrong, on a specific case, with a named owner. A person opens their queue and works down it — they never have to reconstruct the state of the office from memory.

Buckets can still exist as saved views over this — “show me everything in Lokpal” is a filter, not a folder. Habits are preserved; the limitations are not.

Blockers and who they point at

BlockerWho owes the next moveWhat happens automatically
noneUs. This is the backlogSits at the top of the owner’s queue, oldest first
complainantDocuments, consent, signature, feeWhatsApp chases; the referrer is tasked after 7 days
insurerThe 30-day window is runningReminders fall due on days 10, 20 and 30
lokpalOmbudsman — no clock we controlRadar watches the claim mailbox and raises to-dos

One rule to enforce

A case may sit in hold only with a reason and a review date. An open-ended hold is how an eighteen-month case becomes a three-year one.

Design consequence

The checklist is generated from the category, never typed. Each line carries its own state — not asked asked received, checking accepted rejected — and whether a copy will do. The WhatsApp bot already walks a checklist one document at a time; it gets a better list and learns to say “this one must be the original, by post”.

Life, General and anything unusual

Settled — and it solves a problem I could not

Life and General claims use the standard document set for that claim type, plus a free-form request: a staff member types what else is needed and the message reaches the complainant on WhatsApp, appearing as a new line on their checklist like any other.

This is better than the exhaustive matrix I asked for. A fixed list can only be wrong in one of two directions — too long and people ignore it, or too short and it blocks. A standard list plus “ask for anything else” handles the unusual case without anyone maintaining thirty tables. The same escape hatch should exist for Health too, for the case nobody anticipated.

One rule to keep it honest: a free-form request is still a checklist line with a state and an owner — never a message that vanishes into a chat. Otherwise it is not tracked, and untracked asks are how cases go quiet.

The insurer ladder

Taken directly from the escalation paper. A 30-day window with reminders on days 10, 20 and 30, and three ways out, all of which end at the Ombudsman. This runs during Representation — the complainant’s claim mailbox is already live before it starts.

1Ready to represent — documents in, draft approved
2Claim mailbox confirmed — collected at consolidation, checked live here
3Send — to the insurer, from the complainant’s mailbox, forwarding in place
4Tracking — the case is on the clock from the send date
5Acknowledgement — recorded when it arrives; this is the proof the Ombudsman filing needs
630-day window — one of three things now happens

Path A Rejection

  1. Rejection received and recorded
  2. No waiting — eligibility is immediate
→ Escalation

Path B Query received

  1. Query logged; we owe the reply
  2. Reply of query sent
  3. No response — a two-reminder window
  4. Window exhausted
→ Escalation

Path C No reply at all

  1. Day 10 — first reminder
  2. Day 20 — second reminder
  3. Day 30 — third reminder
  4. Window closed
→ Escalation
Reminders: manual by default, automatic if switched on

Each case carries its own setting. Default is manual — the system drafts the reminder, schedules it, and puts it in front of a named person to send. A case can be switched to fully automatic where the handler is confident. Either way every reminder is logged with who sent it and when.

Settled — Path B and the human override

Path B’s two reminders are counted like Path C’s, and every reminder on any path is flagged for a human to take over rather than fired blindly. The system schedules and drafts; a person decides whether this particular insurer, on this particular case, should get the standard reminder or something else.

That is the right instinct. A reminder to an insurer is correspondence on a live legal matter — the schedule should be automatic, the words should not.

Two small things to confirm (Q2)
  • If no acknowledgement ever arrives — the clock still runs from the send date, and the missing acknowledgement is itself a flag.
  • Does a partial reply reset the 30 days, or does the window run regardless?

09

Speed, and the clocks that protect it

The target is four to five months end to end. That is a realistic number, and the arithmetic below shows why two to three was not. Everything in this section exists to make the difference visible while there is still time to act on it.

Where the time has to come from

StageTargetWhat makes it slip, and what stops that
Intake → review2–3 daysReviewer backlog. Section 16 sizes the team so this does not queue
Conversion7–10 daysToday this is unbounded — 27 cases prove it. The chase ladder caps it
Consolidation3–5 daysConsent and mailbox. Both chased from day 3
Documentation10–20 daysThe big variable. Originals by post are the long pole — track the consignment
Drafting3–5 daysWriter and approver capacity
Representation30 daysFixed by the ladder — cannot be compressed, only started sooner
Escalation & Lokpal6–12 weeks +Ombudsman timelines, entirely outside our control. All we control is replying to them the same day — and that is worth measuring on its own
Settlement7–14 daysSeven weeks in the sample case. C11.3 exists for this
Why four to five months is the right number

Intake to representation sent, at target, is roughly 25 to 43 days. Add the mandatory 30-day window and a case is two to two and a half months old before the Ombudsman is even an option. Everything after that belongs to the Ombudsman’s calendar, not ours.

So four to five months is achievable and two to three was not — except for cases the insurer settles during the window, which are worth counting separately because they are the cheapest wins we get.

The number to hold the team to is days on our side of the line: intake to representation-sent, and same-day replies to anything the Ombudsman asks. Total elapsed time is mostly a measure of other people’s speed.

The Ombudsman stage — detail still to come

This part is deliberately thin, and will be rebuilt

What this document says about the Ombudsman today is only the outline: register, record the BHP number, file annexures, attend the hearing, record the award. The detailed Lokpal flow is being written by the team and will replace this section.

Please do not spend review time correcting the detail here — it is a placeholder. What is worth commenting on is whether the surrounding machinery serves it: that Ombudsman mail escalates from day one (R4), that annexure deadlines become tasks automatically (C9.2), that hearing dates reach both the handler and the complainant (C9.3), and that the one-year filing window is counted from day one rather than remembered.

The filing window

The clock nobody watches

A complaint must reach the Ombudsman within one year of the insurer’s rejection. A case can be progressing perfectly — documents in, draft written, insurer corresponding — while that window quietly closes. Then the route is gone.

It should be a countdown on the case header from day one, amber at 90 days and red at 30. At a four-to-five month pace it will rarely bind — which is exactly why it will be forgotten, and exactly why it must be automatic.

10

Human in the loop

The line proposed here is simple and absolute: automation never speaks to an authority, never decides a case, and never tells a complainant an outcome. Everything else it may do freely — and should, because that is what “the system drives” means.

A named person decides

  • The review verdict — can fight or no scope
  • Case category, confirming the AI’s suggestion
  • Accepting a document the AI is unsure about
  • Gist and draft approval
  • Sending anything to an insurer or the Ombudsman, including reminders unless the case is switched to automatic
  • Committing to the Ombudsman — the ladder proposes, a person commits
  • Moving a case forward without a fee (C3.5)
  • Telling a complainant any outcome, good or bad
  • Verifying settlement amounts and approving every share
  • Waiver, refund, withdrawal, deletion

The system does

  • Generate the checklist from the category
  • Chase the complainant for the next document
  • Screen documents for wrong type or poor quality
  • Watch every clock — days 10, 20, 30, the filing window, every SLA
  • Draft the reminder and put it in front of a person
  • Classify incoming email and file it against a case
  • Create tasks and route them to the right queue
  • Send factual status updates
  • Raise flags and never let them go quiet
  • Propose the next step, never take it
The test for anything added later

If it went wrong, would we need to name the person who did it? If yes, a human does it. The AI is good at noticing and organising, and it is not accountable.

Identity and validation

The single highest-leverage change in this document. Almost every stall downstream — the 27 unconverted cases included — traces back to not being able to reach someone.

What must be true before a claim leaves intake

IDFactHow it is establishedBlocking?

V3Complainant emailEmail OTP. Every insurer and Ombudsman message lands hereyes

Why OTP on both, everywhere

A verified mobile means the WhatsApp bot can actually drive the process. A verified email means correspondence never disappears. Both take one extra step at the one moment the complainant is most motivated — right after deciding to come to us. Every later stall costs far more than that step.

Every door, without exception: the website form, the branch walk-in screen, the advisor link, the ₹499 flow, the WhatsApp bot, the CRM lead conversion. One shared validation routine, so no channel can create an unreachable case.

The trade-off, stated honestly

Validation adds friction at exactly the moment a walk-in complainant is standing at a branch counter with a staff member typing for them. Two mitigations: the OTP can be completed by the complainant on their own phone while they are still there, and a branch can save a partial claim as a draft that cannot advance until validation completes — so nothing is lost, but nothing unverified moves either.

Why this is the right bet for aggressive marketing

A complainant arriving from an advertisement has no account, no password and often no patience for a web form. WhatsApp costs them nothing to start and nothing to learn. Everything is created behind the scenes — the account, the claim, the checklist — and they never see it unless they want to.

Three constraints to design around
  • The 24-hour window. Free-form replies only work for 24 hours after they last wrote. Anything later needs an approved template — so every proactive nudge in this document needs a template approved in advance, or it silently fails.
  • Verification gates the private half. The bot already runs public-by-default and will not discuss a case until the complainant proves who they are. That is correct and stays.
  • One number, growing volume. At 30–40 claims a day the quality rating matters. Templates people ignore get the number restricted, so send fewer and better.
Recommendation

Build it as one journey with two surfaces: WhatsApp owns the conversation, the dashboard owns documents-at-length, signing and reading. Every dashboard link sent over WhatsApp should log the person in automatically — asking someone to remember a password is where a WhatsApp-first journey usually dies.

The claim mailbox and the Radar

Settled

The complainant creates a new email address specifically for the claim and shares it with us. We correspond with the insurer and the authorities from it, on their behalf. This is deliberate: authorities expect the complainant to reach them directly, and a visible consultancy domain invites refusal. It is also a purpose-made account holding only claim correspondence — not their personal email.

It is collected at consolidation, well before the representation goes out, so it can never be the thing holding up a case that is already on a clock.

The Radar, two lanes

Lane 1 · Insurance company

Matched by claim number, policy number, or the claim mailbox.

Classified as acknowledgement · query · document request · approval · rejection · other.

Drives the ladder — a query moves the case to Path B, a rejection to Path A, an acknowledgement is recorded as the proof the Ombudsman filing needs.

Lane 2 · Ombudsman office

Matched by the BHP number, unique per complaint.

Classified as registration confirmation · annexure request · hearing notice · document request · award · dismissal.

Creates a dated to-do — a hearing notice becomes a calendar entry with reminders, an annexure request a task with its deadline.

Never auto-reply. The Radar reads, classifies, files and raises work. A human writes every reply — there is an authority on the other end.

Fees, GST and commission

Payment glitches have come from many intake channels and one loose set of rules. The fix is not more checks — it is one path in, one fee model, and a name against every decision.

What each kind of complainant pays

Settled — subscription is the product

An active subscriber’s claim flows straight through: no review fee, no L2 fee, nothing to chase, no conversion step to stall in. The only money is the settlement fee at the end.

That is a cleaner business than the direct funnel, and the system should make the difference visible — a subscriber’s claim should never appear in the conversion queue at all, because there is nothing to convert.

Settled — the review fee is not refundable

A direct client pays first, the review happens, then the case moves to L2. If they withdraw at that point the review fee is not returned — the work was done and delivered.

This needs to be said before they pay, in plain words, on the payment screen and in the receipt. A fee that is non-refundable and unexplained is the single most common source of a complaint against a firm like ours. Said clearly up front, it is simply the terms.

The complainant is the counterparty for everything

Settled — and it simplifies a great deal

Whoever talks to us, sends the documents and signs the authorisation is the complainant — and they are the person liable for our fee. In almost every case that is either the insured acting for themselves, or a family member acting for the insured. The insured is a name on the policy; the complainant is the person we have a relationship with.

So there is one counterparty throughout: one person to verify, one person to chase, one person who signs, one person who pays. No case needs a second signatory and no case stalls waiting to work out who is entitled to commit.

Why this makes the last mile collectable

The hardest moment in the whole process is C11.4 — the complainant has been paid by the insurer and goes quiet before paying us. This settles who that obligation belongs to: a named person, with a phone and email we verified at intake, who signed an authorisation showing the exact amount they would owe. That is a position worth being in. Without it, the same moment is an argument about who agreed to what.

The authorisation is also the fee agreement

This changes the sequence, not just the wording

The authorisation letter must carry the actual number: “as per your disputed claim amount with the insurance company, this is the tentative calculation” — the disputed amount, 15%, the GST on it, and the total the complainant will owe after the claim settles, whether by Ombudsman award or consumer forum.

Which means the disputed amount must be captured and validated before the authorisation can be generated. It cannot be a field someone fills in later. The order is: capture the disputed amount → validate it against the rejection letter and policy → then generate the authorisation with the calculation on it → then take consent.

The claim form needs reworking for this too — the disputed amount stops being one field among many and becomes the number the entire commercial relationship rests on.

Why this is worth the extra step

A complainant who signs knowing they will owe roughly ₹22,000 on a ₹1,50,000 settlement does not dispute the invoice five months later. A complainant who signs a generic authorisation and is billed at the end has every reason to argue — and that argument arrives exactly when we are least able to do anything about it, because the money is already in their account (C11.4).

The tentative calculation is not a legal nicety. It is the thing that makes the last mile collectable.

The fee applies however the claim settles

Settled

15% + GST is due on any settlement — whether the insurer simply pays during the 30-day window, the grievance officer resolves it, the Ombudsman awards it, or a consumer forum does. The work that produced the outcome is the same work.

This also removes an incentive that would have pointed the wrong way. The best result for a complainant is usually the insurer paying quickly and quietly; that must not be the outcome the firm earns least from. It is worth counting those wins separately on the dashboard — they are the cheapest and fastest revenue in the business.

Every payment shows base and GST

Settled

Every payment is base price + GST, shown separately — on the screen, in the receipt, and in the ledger row. The gateway deducts GST automatically once it is passed through, but the split must be explicit everywhere rather than inferred, so a receipt can always be reconciled and a settlement fee can always be explained.

The same applies to the settlement fee: 15% of the settled amount, plus GST on that 15% — never a single blended figure.

Who pays the L2 fee

Settled

Whoever brings the case pays the L2 fee. A case that has not been paid for does not advance on its own. A super-admin or sub-super-admin may move it forward anyway — and when they do, that decision is recorded against their real name, with the fee attributed to them, even if they were impersonating another user at the time.

This mirrors the existing “move to ClaimShield” action, which already records who moved a case. The same accountability applies to moving a case to the next level.

Ending a case

Three ways a case ends other than winning. All three need to be as deliberate as the work that came before.

Abandoned by the complainant

Settled — and this is good practice

If a complainant does not respond to 3 manual calls from staff and 5 WhatsApp reminders across 15 days, the claim is treated as withdrawn. It is archived for 60 days, then the claim itself is deleted — keeping only the basics: name, phone, email, why they came, and the insurance company.

This is real data minimisation. Medical records, bills and discharge summaries for a case nobody is pursuing are pure liability. Deleting them on a schedule is the right instinct, and worth saying so to complainants — it builds exactly the confidence the process needs.

IDPointWhat happens
W1Calls and reminders exhaustedCase marked withdrawn with the evidence attached — the 3 call logs and 5 reminders, so the decision is auditable
W2Before archivingA final message on both channels: “we are closing this, reply to keep it open”. One last chance, clearly given
W3Archived, day 0–60Fully restorable if they come back. Hidden from every queue
W4Day 60Documents and case detail deleted. The basics are kept so a returning complainant is recognised
W5Any fee already paidNot refunded. The review was done and delivered; the L2 fee was paid to start work that started. Stated up front at the point of payment, so this is the terms rather than a surprise

Lost at the Ombudsman

Settled

After a loss we ask whether the complainant wants to go to the consumer forum. We support them legally; the charge is set case by case by a super-admin and collected through the normal payment path. The pipeline therefore does not end at “lost” — it has one more branch, and the system should keep the case open rather than closing it prematurely.

Historic ClaimShield cases

Settled

Cases already running in ClaimShield finish there. NidaanPartner handles all NP cases. No migration of live matters — which is the right call: moving a case mid-Ombudsman risks losing the thread of correspondence that the complaint depends on.
