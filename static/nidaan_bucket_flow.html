Buckets that know their neighbours

You asked how a new bucket joins the flow — what sits above it, what sits below it, what happens when a claim lands in it, and what it must carry before it leaves. Here is what the system already knows, the one piece it is missing, and what I think we should and should not build.

01

The order already IS the flow

This is the part worth knowing before we design anything, because it means we are closer than it looks.

Every bucket carries a sort_order. When somebody moves a claim, the engine works out whether that is a step forward or a step back by comparing the two positions — nothing else. So the sequence of buckets is not decoration. It is the flow, and it already decides what "onward" and "backwards" mean everywhere in the app.

What that gives us for free

Drag a bucket up or down and you have redefined the flow. The move buttons, the direction of every arrow, the "→ Pending Draft" wording on the button, the off-the-usual-path recording — all of it follows automatically, because they all read the same number.

So the missing piece is small and specific: the Bucket Designer never lets you change that number. The column exists, the API accepts it, and the screen has no control for it. That is the gap you felt.

02

Order is the spine. Routes are shortcuts.

Right now there are two separate ideas doing overlapping jobs, and I think that is a mistake we should correct before adding anything on top.

TodayWhat it doesProblem
sort_orderDecides what counts as forward vs back Not editable in the designer
The routes tableDecides which buttons are offered first Separate from the order, so the two can disagree and nobody notices
Proposal

The order defines the default flow. You never draw the main line by hand. The bucket above you is your "send back"; the bucket below you is your "move on". Both are computed, both rename themselves automatically when you drag things around, and neither can ever get out of step with the other.

Routes become only what they are actually good for: shortcuts. "From Pending Documents you can also jump straight to Lokpal." That is a real thing an office needs, and it is a different thing from the main line.

Why this matters for reliability, which is the part you care about most: one idea that is always true beats two ideas that are usually consistent. The second kind is where glitches live. We have already been bitten twice this week by exactly that shape of problem — two places holding the same fact and quietly disagreeing.

The line I want to hold

Nothing on that list sends anything to a customer on its own. The bucket can put the document request in front of a person, filled in and ready; a person still reads it back and presses send. An office where messages leave without a human deciding is an office that cannot answer "who sent this?" — and that is the question we just spent two days making answerable.

04

What I would build, in order

PieceWhy this order
ADrag to reorder, and show above/below on every bucket Smallest change, biggest gain. It makes the flow visible and editable, and every move button renames itself for free. Nothing else depends on it, so it cannot break anything else.
BRoutes become shortcuts only Removes the chance of the two disagreeing. Existing routes that match the main line get absorbed; the rest stay as shortcuts.
COn-arrival actions, from the fixed list Now the flow is stable, giving a bucket a job is safe. Document-collection first, since that is the one with a screen already built.
DExit conditions incl. "documents complete" Last, because it only makes sense once C exists.

Each one ships on its own and is useful on its own. If we stop after A, the system is better and nothing is half-built.

05

On the errors you have been hitting

You are right to push on this, and I want to answer it properly rather than promise harder.

Three things reached you this week that should not have. They were not random — they were three of a kind:

What you hitUnderneath
NP-112 "fee not paid" when it was paid Two places held the same fact and disagreed
"Pick a way to send it" with both ticked A screen read state from a part of itself that had already been replaced
Popups with no close button A baseline nobody had ever checked for
The honest reason

My tests exercise the engine — the rules, the database, the endpoints. All three of those bugs lived above the engine, in the screen. The engine tests passed on every one of them, including the one where I fixed NP-112 and the fix still did not work on the real claim, because the test built its data differently from the way the endpoint does.

So the remedy is not "be more careful". It is to test the thing you actually touch:

  • Drive the real screens — a script that opens the ops page in a real browser, clicks through the handover, the document window and a bucket move, and fails if any button does nothing. This is the one that would have caught all three.
  • One fact, one place — a check that fails the build if the same business rule is written twice. NP-112 was that exact shape, and so was the duty-roster vocabulary.
  • A baseline pass on every dialog — the close-button audit I ran is a script now, not a memory. It runs every time.

I would like to build the first one before we start on buckets, not after.

Answered — 13 Sep

Decided

Q1 Order is the flow. Drag to arrange; above and below are decided for you.
Q2 A bucket CAN genuinely fork. Completed → Pending Payment or CP Payment is a real decision, not a shortcut.
Q3 Prepare, never send. An on-arrival action puts the message in front of a person.
Q4 Dragging is allowed, with preventive action — see below.
Q5 The browser flow test is built first.

What Q2 changes

A fork is not the same as a shortcut, and the screen must not make them look alike. So:

  • The order still gives the default next bucket — the one the button offers when there is nothing to decide.
  • A bucket may declare additional real nexts. When it has more than one, the single "→ Pending Draft" button becomes a short list of equal choices, each named, and the person picks. No default is pre-selected, because a fork means somebody has to decide and a pre-selected answer is how a decision gets made by accident.
  • Shortcuts stay visibly different — they sit under "somewhere else", the way they do today. A fork is part of the flow; a shortcut is a departure from it.
Which means

Completed would declare two nexts: Pending Payment and CP Payment. Staff in Completed see two buttons, both named, neither pre-chosen — and whichever they press still carries the comment every move carries.

The guard rails Q4 asked for

You are right that a careless drag could disturb live work, so dragging is not just allowed and hoped for. Four things, in the order they take effect:

GuardWhat it does
1Some positions are structural and simply cannot move The entry bucket stays first. The terminal bucket stays last. A park bucket sits outside the line entirely — it is a pause, not a step. Dragging these is refused with the reason, because they are not preferences, they are what the engine means by "beginning", "end" and "paused".
2You see the consequence before you commit Let go of a bucket and nothing is saved yet. A panel says exactly what changes: "Pending Documents holds 4 claims. After this, moving them on sends them to Reimbursement instead of Pending Draft." Save or cancel. Live work is never rearranged by a gesture alone.
3One-click undo, for a day Every save keeps the previous order. "Put it back the way it was" restores it exactly, with no thinking required — which is what you want at the moment you realise you have dragged something badly.
4Claims never move; only the bucket does A claim stays exactly where it is. What changes is where it goes NEXT, and the claim shows that plainly rather than hiding it.
The one thing I will not add

A rule that refuses a reorder because claims are in the way. That would mean the busier the office gets, the less it can correct its own process — exactly backwards. Show the consequence, make it reversible, and let the person decide.

06

The questions, as asked

Q1
Is the order the flow, or do you want to draw the lines yourself?

My recommendation is the order. Drag to arrange, and above/below is decided for you, with shortcuts on top for the jumps. The alternative — drawing every connection by hand — is more flexible and considerably easier to get into a broken state.

Q2
Can one bucket have more than one "next"?

For example, does Completed go to Pending Payment sometimes and CP Payment other times, as a genuine fork in the road? Or is there always one main line, with the rest as shortcuts a person chooses? This changes the shape of the whole thing, so it is the one I most need answered.

Q3
Should an on-arrival action ever send to a customer without a person?

My strong recommendation is no — prepare it, never send it. But the automatic nudges already do send on their own after three days, so the line is not absolute and I would rather you drew it than assumed it.

Q4
When you drag a bucket, what happens to the claims already in it?

They stay where they are — the bucket moved, not the claims. But that means a claim can suddenly have a different "next". I think that is correct and should simply be shown, not prevented. Agree?

Q5
Do I build the browser flow test first?

It is a day that produces no new feature, and it is the thing that stops you finding these before I do.
